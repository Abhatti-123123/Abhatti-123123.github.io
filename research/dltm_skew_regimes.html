From Trend-Following to Skew Prediction: Empirical Applications of the dLTM Long-Memory Estimator

Empirical tests of whether dLTM regimes carry tradeable information. On a 30-name S&P 500 trend portfolio, a leverage-neutral tilt toward high-memory names raises Sharpe at unchanged risk (Diebold-Mariano p = 0.03). Market-wide memory predicts the next month's left tail of a 31-market futures book, and de-risking on it cut maximum drawdown from -27% to -17%. On four optionable underlyings, forward realized skewness is monotone in dLTM regime and survives controls for Bakshi-Kapadia-Madan implied skew.

Background and motivation

The dLTM estimator from Bhatti (2026a) recovers a finite-sample long-memory parameter from the tail slope of the autocorrelation function of log prices. The companion paper on over-differencing shows on a panel of 484 S&P 500 names that realized return skewness deepens from about -0.14 to -0.46 as one moves from the low-dLTM regime (d < 0.3) to the high-dLTM regime (d ≥ 0.7). The mechanism is mechanical. More persistent return processes accumulate negative shocks inside trending regimes, which concentrates left-tail mass.

The natural follow-up question is whether that regime-conditional skew prediction has practical applications in portfolio construction or options trading. This note presents empirical tests addressing that question.

Application 1: trend-following with memory-conditioned sizing

Setup

A sector-balanced 30-name S&P 500 universe is selected by long history (15+ years) and dLTM coverage (50%+ valid estimates), with three names per sector across ten sectors. The base strategy is my final Carver system: a risk-parity portfolio with long-only trend-strength sizing. An overlay scales each position by the name's current dLTM regime: x1.5 when d ≥ 0.7, x0.5 when d < 0.3, unchanged otherwise. Regime cutoffs match the Paper 2 buckets and all parameters are fixed ex ante. The backtest spans 2000–2025; walk-forward dLTM estimates (2,800-day window) are live from February 2011. Sharpe differentials are tested with Diebold-Mariano on the daily return differential, Newey-West bandwidth 21.

High-d name-days outnumber low-d ones roughly eight to one, so the x1.5 / x0.5 overlay is on average a leverage increase (mean multiplier 1.24). To separate the signal from that leverage, I also run a leverage-neutral version: the multipliers are rescaled to average one across names every day, leaving a pure tilt toward high-memory names at unchanged total risk.

The levered overlay's significant DM statistic is partly leverage: it raises volatility from 13% to 17% for a Sharpe gain of about +0.01. The leverage-neutral tilt adds +0.026 Sharpe at the same risk and remains significant, so the memory signal carries information beyond leverage. The June 2026 version of this note reported lifts of +0.012 (mask: 0.845 to 0.857) and +0.038 (strength: 0.563 to 0.601) for the levered overlay on an earlier trend book; those lifts likewise mixed signal and leverage. The 30-name list here is rebuilt with a fixed rule (three names per sector by dLTM coverage), because the original list was not retained.

Regime behaviour

The gains come from sustained trends that began in high-memory states (post-GFC 2011, late-cycle 2019) and the largest cost is the 2022 rate-hike regime, when high memory coincided with stress rather than continuation. Over the full 2000–2025 sample the levered overlay's Sharpe dips slightly (0.816 to 0.805) only because nothing changes before 2011 and its extra leverage gives the weaker post-2011 period more weight; de-levered to the base's volatility it is 0.823, and the leverage-neutral tilt is 0.830.

Interpretation

Trend-following is short-skew at the position level, and high-memory regimes coincide with stronger directional persistence. Tilting risk toward high-memory names harvests some of that persistence. The effect is real but small on a risk-adjusted basis; most of the levered overlay's extra return is the price of extra exposure.

A note on the inverse direction

In the earlier trend book I separately tested the inverse overlay (high-d to smaller positions), motivated by the intuition that one could hedge the predicted negative skew by reducing exposure. The result was not statistically meaningful (strength: lift +0.08, p = 0.11; mask: lift -0.02, not significant), and maximum drawdown deteriorated in stress regimes (e.g. the COVID crash worsened from -14% to -32%).

Shrinking a long equity position is not a tail hedge, since the smaller position is still long the same risk. Negative skew can be harvested as a premium or hedged as a risk, but at the level of a single equity the hedge needs a tail-protective instrument such as long puts, collars, or VIX exposure sized by the regime indicator. At the level of a diversified multi-asset book, the next section shows that a market-wide memory state does work as a de-risking signal.

Application 1b: market memory as a tail-risk state for a multi-asset book

Averaging walk-forward d* across about 460 S&P 500 names gives a market memory state. Measured against its own one-year average and bucketed into terciles using only past data, it predicts the next month of my 31-market futures book (the same Carver system, Sharpe 1.04 over 2005–2025).

High memory has the most negative next-month skewness under all three state definitions tested (detrended, expanding terciles, rolling 3-year terciles) and with the February–April 2020 COVID window removed. Sharpe is not monotonic across states: the state predicts tail risk, not returns.

As a rule, halving the book the day after the state enters its top tercile cut the 2013–2025 maximum drawdown from -26.9% to -16.9% and improved skewness from -0.85 to -0.56, with Sharpe 1.14 to 1.19 (CAGR 13.2% to 11.9%, because the book runs less risk on average). Across the three state definitions the drawdown falls to between -14% and -17%. Evidence covers 2013–2025 and high-memory months cluster in 2014–2020, so this is a promising result rather than a settled one.

Application 2: skew prediction on optionable underlyings

If the Application 1 result is driven by the predicted skew mechanism, then dLTM should also predict realized skew directly on individual underlyings, and ideally beyond what the options market already prices via implied skew. This section tests that claim using OptionsDX end-of-day chains for four liquid optionable names.

What did not replicate (options test)

Three caveats apply to the options test and should be stated plainly.

(i) Per-name precision. Zero of four names individually pass a pre-committed t < -1.5 threshold on the d_high coefficient. The result is detectable at the cross-sectional level but not at the single-name level. This is consistent with the mechanism living in cross-sectional differences, where Paper 2 originally documented it, rather than in individual-name time-series predictability.

(ii) Subperiod stability on SPY. Splitting the SPY sample at 2017-01-01, the linear-specification dLTM coefficient is -0.46 (t=-1.05) pre-2017 and -0.26 (t=-0.13) post-2017. The full-sample t-statistic on linear specification (-2.27) reflects pooling, not stable predictability across time.

(iii) Regime imbalance. The 2016-2023 sample sits almost entirely in high-dLTM territory for mega-cap technology names. Inference about low-d regimes from this sub-panel is structurally limited.

Despite these caveats, the result is consistent with Application 1: dLTM-conditioned skew effects are real but require cross-sectional aggregation (across names or across regimes) to be detected with high confidence. Single-name, single-time-series applications are too noisy.

Strategy implications

Taking the applications together, three strategies are potentially viable.

(A) Leverage-neutral memory tilt (equity trend book). Tilt risk toward high-memory names and away from low-memory names at unchanged total risk. A small but significant risk-adjusted gain on a 30-name S&P 500 trend portfolio (Sharpe +0.026, p = 0.03). Suitable as a sizing rule inside an existing trend book; the levered x1.5 / x0.5 version adds return mainly by adding exposure.

(A2) Market-memory de-risking switch (multi-asset book). Cut the book's risk when market-wide memory is high. On a 31-market futures trend book it roughly halved maximum drawdown and improved skewness at equal or better Sharpe (Application 1b).

(B) Cross-sectional regime-contrast skew portfolio (options). Long realized-skew exposure on low-d names (long OTM put plus short OTM call, delta-hedged), short realized-skew exposure on high-d names. Equal-vega sizing, 30-day tenor rolling weekly. The trade is structurally a long-short factor portfolio that exploits the pooled regime contrast shown in the regime-ordering table, not a single-name skew bet. Expected gross Sharpe before transaction costs is in the 0.4 to 0.7 range; net Sharpe after realistic OTM single-name options bid-ask costs (0.5% to 1.5% per round trip) is uncertain.

Construction of (B) requires a larger underlying universe (20 to 30 names) than the four tested here. Expanding the underlying panel is the natural next step and the gating decision for whether the options strategy is worth full backtest construction. A separate extension involves applying dLTM as a sizing signal on a tail-protective options overlay (long puts, collars, VIX longs) rather than on the underlying equity position. The negative result in the defensive test reflects the wrong instrument, not the wrong signal.
