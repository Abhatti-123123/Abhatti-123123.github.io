Long Memory and Crash Risk

Long memory in prices flags crash risk the options market doesn't price.

Paper (PDF) → Slides (PDF) →

Abstract

Long memory in prices, measured with the finite-sample detectability estimator of Bhatti (2026a), identifies when equity crash risk is elevated. On the S&P 500 from 2006 to 2026, a state defined by high intraday memory together with price stress makes a 3% daily loss 4.4 times as likely on the next day. Within stressed markets, high memory still separates the dangerous days. Volatility explains part of the raw effect, but with realized volatility and the VIX controlled, high memory inside stress still multiplies the odds of a 3% loss within 15 days by 2.5 (4.4 out of sample), and the direction holds when the bar grid is shifted or memory is read earlier in the day. At the 36-day horizon, memory flags when a decline, once under way, is likely to persist. The risk is only partly priced: in the state, at-the-money implied volatility rises but put skew does not. Used to cut a portfolio of risk premia, the signal reduced out-of-sample maximum drawdown from 16.7% to 13.8% and beat every constant hedge.

Where it sits

This is the third paper in a series. Paper 1 develops the memory estimator; Paper 2 shows memory regimes structure return forecastability; this paper asks what memory regimes imply for tail risk.

Method in brief

Memory is the S&P 500's own d* on its last 2,789 five-minute bars of regular US hours, re-estimated after each close. The state is on when memory is in the top third of its trailing year and price shows stress (below the 50-day mean, or 21-day volatility above 63-day volatility), and is held for 15 trading days. Every test is compared with 2,000 circular shifts of the state's dates; rules were fixed on 2004–2015 and run once on 2016–2026. The paper also reports where the signal fails: developed Europe and Switzerland, where the available stress signals are policy-driven trends rather than fear signals.
